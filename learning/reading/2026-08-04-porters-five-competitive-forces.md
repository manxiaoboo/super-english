# Reading Session: Porter's Five Competitive Forces
Date: 2026-08-04
Source: 商业英语教材（Business English textbook reading passage）
Difficulty: 中级

## Source Text

Michael Porter's famous Five Forces Model provides a simple but powerful tool for assessing and analysing the competitive forces which influence profitability in an industry.

"Understanding the competitive forces, and their underlying causes, reveals the roots of an industry's current profitability while providing a framework for anticipating and influencing competition (and profitability) over time," says Porter.

**Understanding the five forces**

In Porter's model, the five forces that shape industry competition are as follows.

*Competitive rivalry*

This force examines how intense the competition is in the marketplace, which is determined by the number and capability of your competitors.
Rivalry is measured by the Concentration Ratio (CR). A high CR indicates that a large market share is held by a few firms, so the market is concentrated and less competitive. And it is likely that you can have a fair profit margin. A low CR indicates that the industry is characterised by many rivals, thus the market is fragmented and competitive.

*Supplier power*

Here you assess how much power your supplier has and how easy it is for your supplier to raise its prices, which, in turn, would lower your profitability.
Supplier power comes from the number of available suppliers, the uniqueness of their products / services, the cost of switching from one supplier to another, and so on.

*Buyer power*

This force looks at the power of the buyer to affect pricing. If there are many sellers but a few buyers, or if it is easy for buyers to switch from one business's product / service to another's, the buyer is likely to set the price. Buyer power is low when there are many buyers and they purchase products / services in small amounts, or when they can hardly find substitutes for your product / service.

*Threat of new entry*

This force examines the ability of competitors to enter your market. If it costs little in terms of time or money to enter your market and to compete with you, if there are few economies of scale, or if you have little protection for your key technologies, it is easy for competitors to take market share away from you and weaken your position.
Barriers to entry also include well-recognised brands, restricted distribution channels, high capital costs etc.

*Threat of substitution*

This force studies how easy it is for consumers to switch from your product / service to that of a competitor. The extent of the threat depends upon how your competitors' prices and quality compare to yours, customer loyalty, switching costs etc. If your product / service faces a threat of substitution, you will have to reduce the costs and therefore prices to gain a price advantage over your competitors or to make your product / service differentiated from that of competitors'.

**Strategies for success**

Once you analysed competitive strength of your company, it is time to find a strategy to enhance your competitive advantage.

*Cost leadership*

This strategy calls for being the low-cost producer in an industry for a given level of quality. The company can sell its product / service either at the average industry price to increase profits, or below the average industry price to gain market share.

*Differentiation*

This strategy involves making the company's product / service significantly better than or different from competitors, therefore allowing the companies to charge a premium price for it.

*Focus*

This strategy encourages companies to concentrate on a narrow segment of the market and satisfy the unique needs of customers within it. By doing so, a company can enjoy a high degree of customer loyalty, which discourages other companies from competing.
